### Building the four-step DAWN product vertical

Prometheus is AmadeusWeb's IT Product and Services wing.

Its product vertical takes an organisation from being understood and discovered to operating within a connected, secure, capable digital ecosystem.

The four steps are **Marketing, Federation, Security, and CRM / ERP**.

They are not disconnected service labels.

They describe a progression: communicate the mission, connect it to others, protect the people and work involved, and support the relationships and operations that sustain delivery.

### 1. Marketing: make the mission clear and discoverable

Marketing begins with understanding.

An organisation must be able to explain who it is, what it offers, whom it serves, and what action a visitor can take.

My work across web applications, global sales pages, publishing systems, content architecture, and client projects gives me a builder's view of this journey.

At Photon, I was lead developer on the Englishtown sales-page work.

The pages were unified and revamped across global markets.

The work included redesigning pages, fixing defects, creating dynamic pages, setting up environments, onboarding, and supporting the release process.

This is direct experience with the practical realities behind a marketing presence: multiple audiences, consistent presentation, dynamic content, and dependable releases.

I later built websites and publishing tools through freelance work.

Those projects included sports communities, timesheet applications, custom frameworks, business-to-business commerce, booking workflows, publishing, and WordPress extensions.

At BORN Group, I worked on product advertising and a catalog migration.

I assessed alternatives, ported selected features, benchmarked frameworks, understood a Magento schema, and built a WinForms tool to prepare catalog data for import.

These are not abstract marketing credentials.

They are the technical foundations that make offers discoverable, pages maintainable, content portable, and customer journeys implementable.

I know that a landing page is not the whole marketing system.

Content structure, metadata, audience intent, a clear next step, and operational follow-through matter just as much.

Spring supports this approach with file-based pages, Markdown, HTML, PHP, sheets, metadata, folder-derived navigation, media, and reusable templates.

The aim is not to force every organisation into one campaign style.

The aim is to give each mission a clear, owned home and a path from interest to meaningful action.

### 2. Federation: connect independent homes and capabilities

Federation demands more than links between websites.

It requires sound architecture, clearly bounded responsibilities, interoperability, discoverability, and respect for each participant's autonomy.

My career has repeatedly involved building frameworks that let different components work together without making every project identical.

At Applied Development, I worked on ERP, financial, HR/legal, service-management, documentation, and internal workflow projects.

I led development on a TopPower ERP framework with object-relational and UI mappers.

I co-architected framework capabilities and built a legacy code explorer to track conversion work.

That experience taught me how to make a common foundation useful across distinct operational contexts.

At Sulekha, I wrote architecture and infrastructure layers for a mobile consumer-to-service-provider matching platform.

The stack included VueJS, TypeScript, and ASP.NET Core.

I also began architecting and proving a scalable Communications Center that integrated providers such as WhatsApp and Google My Business.

That work required thinking about multiple services, multiple channels, and a coherent experience across them.

At BORN Group, I worked across .NET and PHP during a product migration.

I ported selected features to different PHP frameworks and built statistics modules to compare their suitability.

I was the developer who knew both languages and helped the existing team learn PHP.

That is practical federation work at the engineering level: preserving useful capabilities while changing boundaries and connecting different technologies.

Spring applies this mindset to the web.

Each site can have its own domain, content, identity, and purpose.

Folder-based structure makes navigation and relationships visible.

Shared media can be served through a CDN without bloating each site's own content.

Public sites can publish from their own repositories, while the network connects them through human-readable links and shared conventions.

Federation should strengthen each member's home, not absorb it into an opaque central platform.

My framework and integration experience gives me the judgment to design for that balance.

### 3. Security: make responsibility part of the design

Security is not a decorative feature or a slogan.

It is a set of choices about data, access, deployment, maintenance, recovery, and who is accountable.

I have worked on an intranet information-security application, banking backends, enterprise systems, internal tools, and global web products.

At Constient, I worked on the backend of a banking site and identified performance bottlenecks.

I mentored teams, worked with legacy code, and built a ticket generator with barcode printing.

At Applied Development, my work included HR/legal systems, an intranet application, and a custom code-coverage analysis tool.

In other roles, I have handled code reviews, troubleshooting, release processes, deployment environments, and technical standards.

These experiences have taught me to treat security as an operational responsibility.

A system must be understood by the people who maintain it.

Its data boundaries must be deliberate.

Its deployment and recovery practices must be known, not assumed.

Its risks must be discussed in the context of how it is actually used.

Spring takes a deliberately lightweight approach to publishing.

Content is stored as files and can be backed up with Git.

Work can be developed offline, reviewed locally, and deployed through a controlled upload process.

The framework avoids requiring a database-backed login for ordinary public publishing.

That reduces entire categories of operational complexity for sites that do not need those capabilities.

It does not mean every business workflow has no security needs.

Private records, authenticated functions, payment flows, and external integrations require appropriate safeguards and careful design.

My experience helps distinguish where simpler architecture is a security advantage and where stronger controls are necessary.

### 4. CRM / ERP: connect relationships to delivery and operations

CRM and ERP systems are where promises meet real work.

They must support the way an organisation receives enquiries, manages relationships, plans delivery, records operations, and learns from outcomes.

I have spent years building in and around these systems.

At Applied Development, I contributed to ERP applications and loan-application and servicing frameworks.

I built custom object-relational mappers and UI frameworks for Windows applications.

I delivered service-management, financial, HR/legal, timesheet, and candidate-processing workflows.

At Photon, I worked on application and database concerns for a large international learning product.

At Headstream, I led a small team across frontend and backend technologies and delivered implementations as a one-person team during a transition.

At Sulekha, I worked on a matching platform that connected consumers and service providers.

I have also built reporting systems.

In 2018, I wrote a generic flexible reporting engine in C#.

At Headstream, I wrote a robust and generic reporting framework.

At Sulekha, I documented processes, checklists, plans, requirements, work breakdown structures, technical specifications, features, and bugs in an organised Git repository.

That combination matters to CRM and ERP.

I understand that a contact record is not useful in isolation.

It must connect to an agreed next step, a responsible person, an opportunity or service, and a record of what happened.

An enquiry can lead to a conversation.

A conversation can become an agreement.

An agreement can create a project, service task, or operational commitment.

Delivery can then inform follow-up, reporting, and improvement.

Spring provides a flexible web and content foundation for the public-facing parts of this path.

Where an organisation needs private CRM or ERP workflows, the right answer may be a purpose-built extension, a specialist product, a documented integration, or a staged combination.

I have built custom business frameworks and I know when to reuse an established tool.

The objective is a coherent workflow, not rebuilding every commodity system for its own sake.

## AmadeusWeb Spring: a treasure chest of practical possibilities

Spring is not an off-the-shelf promise that every imaginable application is already complete.

It is a mature, extensible foundation for building a wide range of web experiences and digital workflows.

It brings together ordinary, inspectable technologies with a proprietary framework that stitches the site experience together.

The platform supports Markdown, HTML, PHP, TSV sheets, rich pages, forms, decks, email templates, AI chat exports, images, PDFs, and embedded media.

The exact combination depends on the mission and the job to be done.

Content and code can be organised in folders that express the site's structure.

Top-level sections can stay out of URLs while nodes, subnodes, pages, and contextual menus provide meaningful navigation.

The menu can be derived by reading the folders and files.

Article sheets can feed landing pages while each article carries its own metadata.

This keeps content and presentation inspectable and maintainable.

PHP, TSV, and HTML allow rich pages to separate content from layout in different ways.

Themes and reusable templates help produce consistent sites.

Media can live in site folders or in shared CDN locations.

This gives teams room to build without allowing every website to become an unstructured pile of assets.

Git provides history and a practical backup path for file-based content.

Teams can develop offline and review work before publishing it.

VS Code, TortoiseGit, and project-management practices give collaborators tools they can learn and use directly.

The public repository model makes the work transparent for projects that choose public publishing.

Structured email templates let a visitor open an email draft rather than sending information through a hidden form-processing service.

The platform can use a simple FTP deployment path for sites whose requirements suit it.

More complex requirements can be assessed and handled through appropriate code, integrations, infrastructure, or specialist services.

This is the treasure of Spring: not a claim that one feature fits every case, but a versatile set of building blocks that can be combined around the real requirement.

A marketing site, knowledge base, directory, service catalogue, publishing workflow, documentation hub, or mission network can start from the same comprehensible foundation.

The site can grow iteratively as the organisation learns.

That is why I describe a website as a three-year outline of content, activities, and areas of work, built step by step.

The principle is captured in my own words:

> “So a website is really a 3 year outline of what sorts of content is needed, to support which areas and activities and then built iteratively.”
